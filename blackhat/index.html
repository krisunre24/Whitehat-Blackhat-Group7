Black Hat Visualization Write-Up

For the black hat visualization, I wanted to show how the same real data can leave a very different impression depending on how it’s presented. I didn’t change a single OECD number. Instead, I played with the scale, the wording, and which parts of the graph catch your eye first. The goal was to make the long-term change look smaller and steadier than it really is when you look at the whole trend.

One of the main choices I made was using a y-axis from 0 to 25 gigatonnes of CO₂-equivalent. The actual values only take up a smaller part of that range, which makes the changes over time look much flatter than they really are. I also made the 1990 and 2022 endpoints stand out more than the rest of the line and added a light reference line at the 1990 value. This makes it easier for someone to compare the first and last years while paying less attention to what happened in between, including the rise in emissions during the 2000s.

The title, “Greenhouse Gas Emissions Remain Near 1990 Levels,” also pushes the viewer toward that same idea before they look closely at the graph. I kept the subtitle more neutral so the page would still feel like a regular data visualization instead of making the misleading intent too obvious. I also chose OECD, the United States, and OECD America for the dropdown because their 1990 and 2022 values are fairly close compared with many other areas in the dataset. Larger regional totals like OECD also hide some of the bigger differences between individual countries.

Data Source

The data came from the OECD Data Explorer, Air Emissions: Greenhouse Gas Emissions Inventories. I used annual greenhouse gas emissions, specifically total emissions excluding LULUCF, reported in tonnes of CO₂-equivalent. The visualization was built using HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and D3.js.

Development Process

My partner and I split the work. I took the black hat visualization, and my partner took the white hat one. I spent about 15 hours on my side. A lot of that went into digging through the OECD dataset, checking the year ranges and units, comparing different countries and regions, and then building the D3 chart and fixing what broke. The hardest part was working out which design choices would make the chart misleading but still believable. I kept adjusting the scale, the title, the regions I picked, and the visual emphasis until it looked convincing while still pulling attention toward the 1990 and 2022 comparison instead of everything that happened in between.
